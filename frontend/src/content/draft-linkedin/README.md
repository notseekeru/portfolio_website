# LinkedIn drafts

The shape the 2026 studies score best, and why each rule exists. The numbers belong to the studies, so quote them from the source rather than from this page.

- [What Works on LinkedIn, 74,119 posts, per-follower and within-author lenses](https://www.nbksocial.com/research/what-works-on-linkedin/)
- [postiv.ai, 62,747 posts and 1,340 creators](https://postiv.ai/blog/linkedin-post-study-2026)
- [authoredup, 372,126 posts, engagement by character band](https://authoredup.com/blog/linkedin-character-limit)
- [linkpost, 359,000 posts, opener types](https://www.linkpost.gg/en/blog/accroche-hook-linkedin)

## Rules

- **Open with a story anchored in time, or with a number.** Those are the two highest opener bands; a question sits near the bottom and a fragment of three words or fewer below a plain statement. The first 200 characters are all most readers see.
- **One sentence per line, blank line between.** Dense paragraph blocks are the worst-scoring shape, and the line-per-sentence look is already the majority habit among the writers measured. Treat it as the floor, not an advantage.
- **Do not cut for length.** The datasets disagree: one peaks at 2,001 to 2,500 characters with impressions measured, another indexes 700 to 2,000 best per follower, a third finds length near neutral. Cut repetition, never substance. 3,000 is the cap.
- **Upload the images.** A link card is the weakest format in every dataset that measures it. A bare URL costs less when it sits near the end, and nothing at all when it stays out of the body.
- **No hashtags.** One study puts them at a cost, the other finds no effect, and neither finds a gain.

## What is not a format problem

Formatting is saturated, so it cannot differentiate. The content is what carries a post: a number nobody else has, an admission, a diagram that answers a question the reader was about to ask.
